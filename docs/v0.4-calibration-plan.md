# v0.4 — Guided calibration

> **Status: complete, verified on the panel.** Four targets, verification, save
> or retry, stored per display. 227 tests pass.
>
> The check that mattered: the solved range agreed with the hand-calibrated one
> to within five pixels on three edges and thirteen on the fourth. Two
> independent methods converging is what says the arithmetic is right — an error
> in the solver would have differed by multiples, not by pixels.

Per spec §15–16, §28 (v0.4). Replaces "wave your finger about until the range
has been learned" with touching four known points.

## Goal

A borderless overlay on the panel showing targets one at a time, then a
verification screen with a marker following the finger, then Save or Retry.

**Definition of Done** (spec §28): fullscreen overlay, four calibration points,
save/retry, reset, visual verification.

## Why this is more than a UI

The current model learns raw min and max by watching values go by. It cannot
know whether the extremes it saw were the panel's edges or just the furthest the
user happened to reach, and spec §16 asks for calibration derived from *known*
screen points instead. That changes the arithmetic, not only the presentation.

**Targets cannot sit in the physical corners** — a target at the very edge is
half off-screen and cannot be touched accurately. They will sit at a fraction in
from each edge, which means the raw values collected are not the raw extremes
and have to be extrapolated outwards:

```
raw(f) = rawA + (f - fA) · (rawB - rawA) / (fB - fA)
```

Solved at `f = 0` and `f = 1` for each axis. Four corners give two independent
estimates per axis — the top and bottom edges for X, the left and right for Y —
and averaging them is what makes a crooked touch cost less.

That function is pure, is where the accuracy actually lives, and gets tested
first, before any window exists.

## Three decisions to take first

### 1. Touches during calibration must not click

While the overlay is up, a finger on a target would otherwise be translated into
a click on the overlay. The driver needs a mode where frames go to the
calibration controller **instead of** the gesture engine, not as well as it.

Proposed: the driver takes an optional frame observer; while one is set, the
engine is bypassed entirely. That keeps it a diversion rather than a fork, and
the engine cannot half-see a gesture it will never see the end of.

### 2. The overlay has to be on the panel, above everything

`NSWindow` that is borderless, at `.screenSaver` level, sized to the target
display's bounds. Finding the right `NSScreen` means matching
`deviceDescription["NSScreenNumber"]` against the `CGDirectDisplayID` the
display selection already resolves — the two APIs describe the same displays and
do not share a type.

The window must not take key status, or the menu-bar app becomes the active
application and the overlay steals focus from whatever is behind it.

### 3. Calibration becomes per display

Spec §15 asks for it to be stored per device and per display. Today a single
`CalibrationData` lives in `~/.m14ttouch.json`, which is wrong the moment a
second panel appears, and also loses a calibration when a display is swapped.

Proposed: key the store by the display identity introduced in v0.3, with the
existing single value migrated in as the entry for whatever display is current.
Unlike the settings migration, this one is worth writing: people have calibrated
already, and silently discarding it would be a regression they feel immediately.

## Steps

1. **The maths.** `CalibrationSolver`: target samples in, `CalibrationData` out.
   Pure and fully tested — inset targets, extrapolation, a crooked touch,
   degenerate input, inverted axes.
2. **Per-display storage.** Key the store by display identity, migrating the
   existing single value.
3. **The diversion.** Driver frame observer that bypasses the engine, so a
   calibration touch cannot click.
4. **The overlay.** Borderless window on the right display, targets shown one at
   a time, collecting a sample per target.
5. **Verification.** A marker following the finger, with Save and Retry.
6. **Wiring.** A Calibrate button in the settings window and the menu.
7. **Verify on the panel**, including that the result is measurably better than
   the range the descriptor claims.

## Risks

**A calibration that makes things worse.** The current auto-calibration works
well enough on this panel; a solver with an arithmetic error would produce
something plausible-looking and wrong. Step 7 compares against the known-good
saved values rather than only asking whether it feels right.

**Touch during calibration is the whole trick.** If the diversion in step 3
leaks, the overlay gets clicked, targets advance by themselves, and the result
is nonsense. It is the first thing to verify on hardware, before the maths is
judged.

**The overlay on the wrong screen** is a dead end for the user: they cannot see
what to touch, and touching does nothing they can observe. Worth a check that
the resolved `NSScreen` matches the selected display before anything is shown.
